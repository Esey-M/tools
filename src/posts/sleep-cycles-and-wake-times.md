You can sleep for eight hours and wake up feeling wrecked, or sleep for six and wake up fine. The difference is often not how long you slept but **where in a cycle the alarm went off**.

## What a sleep cycle is

Sleep is not uniform. It moves through stages in a repeating pattern, each full cycle lasting roughly 90 minutes — in practice anywhere from 70 to 120, varying between people and across the night.

Within each cycle:

- **N1** — the transition into sleep. A few minutes. Easily woken, and often not experienced as sleep at all.
- **N2** — light sleep. The largest share of the night, around 45–55% of total sleep time.
- **N3** — deep, slow-wave sleep. Hardest to wake from. Concentrated in the first half of the night.
- **REM** — dreaming sleep, with near-paralysed muscles and active brain. Periods lengthen through the night.

The composition shifts as the night goes on. Early cycles are deep-sleep heavy; later cycles are REM-heavy. This is why the last hours of sleep, which people cut first, are not interchangeable with the first hours — they contain most of the night's REM.

## Sleep inertia

Waking from N3 deep sleep produces **sleep inertia**: disorientation, impaired reaction time and poor decision-making. It typically lasts 15–30 minutes and, in studies of sudden waking from deep sleep, cognitive performance immediately on waking can be worse than after a night of no sleep at all.

Waking from N1, N2 or the end of a REM period is far gentler.

This is the whole mechanism behind cycle-based alarm timing. If the alarm lands at 6 hours rather than 6.5, it may catch you in deep sleep, and the 30 minutes of extra sleep will have cost you more than it gave.

## The arithmetic

Working in 90-minute blocks, and allowing roughly 15 minutes to fall asleep:

| Cycles | Sleep | Go to bed for a 07:00 alarm |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 6 h | 00:45 |
| 5 | 7 h 30 | 23:15 |
| 6 | 9 h | 21:45 |

Five cycles — 7.5 hours — sits inside the 7–9 hour range recommended for adults by the US National Sleep Foundation, and is the usual target.

The obvious caveat: **90 minutes is an average, not your number.** If cycle timing consistently seems not to work for you, your cycles may run closer to 80 or 100 minutes. A week of noting when you wake naturally, without an alarm, tells you more than any formula.

## Why the snooze button makes it worse

Nine minutes is not enough for a new cycle. It is enough to re-enter light sleep, which the second alarm then interrupts — restarting sleep inertia without delivering any restorative sleep.

Repeated snoozing produces fragmented sleep at the point in the morning when sleep is most REM-rich. The fix is to set the alarm for when you will actually get up, and get up.

## What matters more than cycle timing

Cycle timing is a marginal optimisation. These are not:

**A consistent wake time**, including weekends. The body clock is set primarily by wake time and light exposure. Sleeping in on Saturday shifts your rhythm as reliably as jet lag does — the effect has a name, "social jet lag", and Sunday-night insomnia is usually its result.

**Morning light.** Ten to thirty minutes of outdoor light shortly after waking anchors the clock more strongly than anything else available. Indoor lighting is roughly 100–500 lux; an overcast morning outdoors is 1,000–10,000.

**Caffeine timing.** Caffeine's half-life is around 5–6 hours, so a 4 pm coffee still has a quarter of its dose active at midnight. It reduces deep sleep even when it does not prevent falling asleep.

**Alcohol.** It shortens the time to fall asleep and then suppresses REM and fragments the second half of the night. Sleep after drinking is reliably worse sleep, even when it is longer.

**Temperature.** Core body temperature has to fall for sleep to begin. A cool room — around 16–19 °C — helps; a warm one is one of the most common causes of poor sleep in summer.

## The honest summary

If you have a fixed wake time, working backwards in 90-minute blocks is a reasonable way to choose a bedtime, and it costs nothing to try. It is genuinely why a 7.5-hour night often feels better than an 8-hour one.

But it is a refinement on top of the basics, not a substitute for them. Consistent timing, morning light and not drinking coffee at four in the afternoon will do far more for how you feel than landing an alarm precisely between cycles.
